Reducing duplicate signatures in subnet propagation

leansim experiments on branch signature-sync · updated 12 November 2026 · Shadow simulations of 32,768 validators in 16 committees

Summary. The best setup we have found so far for propagating validator signatures inside a subnet is a small gossipsub mesh, signatures pushed in bundles, and a rule under which the two ends of each mesh link take turns: for every signature one end pushes it and the other only announces that it holds it. On a network of 32,768 validators in 16 committees of 2,048, this delivers every signature with 0.90 duplicates per first copy instead of 4.34 and sends 67% fewer bytes than plain gossipsub. The slowest hosts get their signatures sooner: 95% of hosts hold 90% of their committee after 1,974 ms instead of 2,505 ms. The median host gets there 184 ms later (1,386 against 1,202 ms); two variants in the conclusion avoid that at the price of more duplicates.

1. What we simulate and what we measure

Each run is one slot: every validator signs once and the signature travels through its committee's subnet. These runs have no aggregation step; they measure propagation only.

  • Network. 32,768 validators in 16 committees of 2,048, one committee per subnet, on 3,713 hosts from a mainnet-like node map: the 2,974 hosts that run validators and 739 relays. Each host subscribes to 2 random subnets, so a subnet has about 464 hosts, and publishes its validators' signatures into their committees' subnets, usually from outside the subnet through fanout peers.
  • Links. Country-based latency. Hosts with at least 10 validators have 1 Gbit/s; the others have 50 Mbit/s up and 100 Mbit/s down. About half of all deliveries go to the fast hosts.
  • Signatures are 1,200 bytes. Propagation appears to be bandwidth-bound: median delivery takes around a second, far longer than link latencies. We infer this from the delivery times; we have not measured queues directly.
  • 34 configurations, one run each, on the server's native Shadow. Every run delivered every signature to every subscriber except one, gossipsub at the mesh-3 setting (section 4).

The node map has 13,613 hosts in all. With every one of them the baseline needed more memory than we may use on the shared server, so these runs keep the validator-running hosts. Earlier, smaller runs are compared in section 8.

Metrics

  • Duplicates per first copy: copies of a signature a host receives after it already holds it, divided by the first copies. This is the headline number.
  • Delivery: time from the first signature sent to a host receiving a signature, over all (host, signature) pairs, for the slower (regular) hosts; median and 95th percentile.
  • 90% of committee: time from the first signature sent until a subscribed host holds 90% of its committee's signatures, which is what an aggregator would need. Median and 95th percentile over the 7,426 host-subnet pairs.
  • Slowest full committee: when the last host-subnet pair held all 2,048 signatures.
  • Wire bytes: signature copies plus all control traffic, summed over hosts (QUIC framing excluded).

2. Why gossipsub produces duplicates

Gossipsub forwards a message to every mesh peer the moment it arrives. Each mesh link therefore carries each signature at least once, and it carries it twice whenever both ends receive it from somewhere else at about the same time and push it at each other.

We added instrumentation to measure this directly (mesh-8 setting):

  • Each mesh link carries a signature 1.69 times on average. About 70% of links carry it in both directions. If every link carried it once, pushed duplicates would fall from 4.26 to 2.12 per first copy.
  • The mesh is smaller than configured. The setting "low 4, target 8, high 12" produces an average degree of 6.17. Degree follows the low mark, probably because gossipsub only grafts up to the target when a host is below the low mark.
  • Mesh forwards cause almost all of it. 4.26 of the 4.34 duplicates per first copy come from mesh forwards and 0.08 from IWANT responses (81% of which arrive already held).
  • IDONTWANT barely acts. It avoided about 19,000 forwards out of 66 million duplicates, because a forward leaves before any IDONTWANT can arrive.
The floor for any scheme that pushes on every mesh link is about half the mesh degree minus one duplicates per first copy. Getting there needs the two ends of a link to stop pushing at each other; going below it needs fewer links that push.

3. EIP-8334 bundles

We started from EIP-8334 as it stood before it moved to waggle: signatures travel over gossipsub's partial-messages extension, which we backported to the vendored gossipsub.

  • Every 20 ms tick a host pushes each mesh peer the signatures that peer is not known to hold, in bundles of up to 50. A signature a peer delivers while the host waits for its tick is not sent back.
  • No metadata goes to mesh peers. At each gossipsub heartbeat a host advertises validator indices to a few non-mesh peers, which request what they lack.

EIP-8334 as specified does not reduce duplicates. Its pushes produce as many duplicates as gossipsub's forwards (4.28 against 4.26 per first copy), and its requests to gossip peers add 0.31 where IWANT adds 0.08.
  • The tick is too short to matter. With a 20 ms tick only 0.5% of pushes are avoided, and even 100 ms avoids 2.6% while delaying the median host by about 125 ms.
  • What the EIP does give is fewer bytes and faster delivery: 12.4 GB of IHAVE, IWANT and IDONTWANT become 1.2 GB of framing and metadata, and hosts hold 90% of their committee about 80 ms earlier at the median and 470 ms earlier at the 95th percentile.
  • Not modelled: the EIP's main saving on mainnet is sharing one AttestationData per bundle. We kept every signature at its full 1,200 bytes, so that saving is not in these numbers.

The mesh-4 setting removes 42% of duplicates and 36% of bytes, and costs the median host about 180 ms. The mesh-3 setting lost signatures under gossipsub. Two of the 7,426 host-subnet pairs ended up with no mesh peers at all and never received their full committee; 86 more had a single mesh peer. With bundles the same two pairs were filled in through requests to gossip peers. The simulation has no packet loss or churn, so real networks would do worse.

We therefore treat the mesh-4 setting as the working point and look for savings that keep its connectivity. Even there, 55 pairs have two mesh peers or fewer and one has a single peer.

5. Second lever: the two ends of a link take turns

To stop the two ends of a link pushing the same signature at each other, we let a hash of the signature and the two peer ids decide which end pushes first. The other end waits: it pushes only if it has held the signature for a stagger delay without the peer delivering it. A host's own signatures are never delayed.

Bundles with turn-taking and no announcements. Stagger 0 is plain EIP-8334 bundles.

  • It removes duplicates, at a price in median latency. At the mesh-4 setting, 400 ms cuts duplicates from 2.65 to 2.21 and delays the median host by about 160 ms; 1,600 ms nearly reaches one copy per link (1.04) at a cost of 535 ms. The 95th percentile barely moves until the longest stagger.
  • Why it needs long delays: a signature takes around a second to cross a subnet, so the end that should push first often does not hold the signature yet. At 400 ms more waiting pushes ran out the delay (15.3 million) than were saved (7.6 million).
  • At the mesh-8 setting an 800 ms stagger removes 22% of duplicates and delays the median host by about 220 ms.
Turn-taking alone forces a trade: a long wait removes duplicates but delays the signatures that only the waiting end can supply. Announcements remove that trade.

6. Third lever: the waiting end announces

The end that waits tells the peer at once that it holds the signature, with an 8-byte validator index in the parts-metadata message the EIP already defines for gossip peers. Panels 3 and 4 of the figure above show the two outcomes:

  • The peer gets the signature elsewhere: it never pushes it back, and announces in turn, which cancels the waiting end's push. The link carries no copy at all, which is below the one-copy-per-link floor.
  • The peer still lacks it: it requests the signature, optionally after a short request delay, and is served. It no longer has to wait out the stagger, so the stagger becomes a fallback only.

Announcements cut duplicates without the stagger's delay. At an 800 ms stagger they take duplicates from 1.90 to 1.42 and bring the median host back from 1,621 to 1,259 ms, slightly ahead of plain bundles. With the request delay the result has fewer duplicates than gossipsub at mesh 3, is faster, and delivers everything.
  • The stagger length stops mattering for latency. With announcements, 400, 800 and 1,600 ms all give a median of about 1,260 ms, so a long fallback is free and saves the most duplicates (1.81, 1.42, 1.15).
  • The request delay is a trade. Waiting 200 ms before requesting removes about 0.25 duplicates per first copy and costs the median host about 125 ms. Without announcements the same delay does little (2.10 against 2.21).
  • Dropping the fallback gains almost nothing (0.90 to 0.88) and hurts the tail: the slowest pair completes at 4,360 ms instead of 3,480 ms. It also removes the only recovery path if a request goes unanswered: a host asks just the first announcer and does not remember the others. We keep the fallback.
  • Cost: announcements add about 0.85 GB, 2.3% of the bytes, because each travels in a small frame of its own (about 28 bytes per announced index). Packing them tighter would shrink that.

Timing depends on the subnet size. In the smaller subnets the best setup was faster across the board: aggregators reached their 90% threshold 520 to 780 ms earlier at the median. With about 464 hosts per subnet the median host is 184 ms slower and only the tail is faster. A mesh of degree four needs more hops to cover a larger subnet, which is the likely reason; we have not measured hop counts.

The earlier runs measured the aggregators' threshold, not the per-host metric used here. The first of them ran in a different Shadow build, which gave somewhat different numbers for the same configuration (4.29 against 4.59 duplicates per first copy), so compare within a row only.

9. What we tried that did not help

  • A longer tick. From 0 to 100 ms it removes about 0.06 duplicates per first copy and delays the median host by about 125 ms.
  • Smaller bundles. One signature per bundle leaves duplicates unchanged, is no faster, and brings back the per-message overhead (114.2 GB against 107.2).
  • Delaying requests to gossip peers on its own: 4.59 to 4.56 with a 200 ms delay.
  • Bitmap pull and coded repair on top of gossipsub, earlier add-ons on this branch: 4.44 and 4.38 duplicates per first copy against 4.34, with more bytes (111.8 and 127.3 GB against 109.8).
  • A suspected cause that turned out negligible: re-sending old signatures to newly joined mesh peers. It did not happen once in these runs, out of 85 million pushes.

10. Conclusion: the best setup so far

Use a small mesh, bundle the pushes, and let the two ends of each link take turns with announcements.

# gossipsub mesh: actual degree about 4.3
gossipsub_mesh_n_low = 3
gossipsub_mesh_n = 4
gossipsub_mesh_n_high = 6

signature_propagation = "bundled"   # EIP-8334 bundles over gossipsub partial messages
bundle_tick_ms = 20
bundle_max_signatures = 50
bundle_stagger_ms = 1600            # the waiting end's fallback push
bundle_announce = true              # the waiting end announces at once
bundle_request_delay_ms = 200       # wait before requesting an announced signature
bundle_request_timeout_ms = 1000

The setup above has the fewest duplicates and bytes, and it trades median latency for them. The median host holds 90% of its committee 184 ms later than under the baseline; the slowest 5% get there 530 ms earlier and the last pair completes 2 s earlier.

Variants

  • If median latency matters more, set bundle_request_delay_ms = 0: 1.15 duplicates per first copy and a median only 58 ms behind the baseline, with the same gain in the tail.
  • If no median slowdown is acceptable, or mesh-8 connectivity is required, keep the mesh-8 setting and add announcements: the median matches the baseline, the tail is the fastest of all, and duplicates and bytes still fall by about half.

What each ingredient contributes

  • The mesh size is the largest single lever: 42% fewer duplicates going from the mesh-8 to the mesh-4 setting, at a cost of about 180 ms for the median host.
  • Announcements with turn-taking are the second: they take the mesh-4 setting from 2.65 to 1.15 duplicates per first copy and are slightly faster than plain bundles.
  • The request delay trims a further 0.25 and costs the median host about 125 ms.
  • Bundling itself does not reduce duplicates, but it carries the other mechanisms, saves bytes and speeds delivery up.

11. Caveats and open questions

  • One seed per configuration. The ranking is stable across three network sizes, but the individual numbers have not been repeated.
  • No loss, churn or misbehaving peers. This matters most for the small mesh and for announcements: a peer that announces a signature and never serves it could delay a neighbour by up to the request timeout. A retry that remembers every announcer is not built yet.
  • Not every host of the node map. These runs use the 3,713 hosts that run validators. With all 13,613 hosts the baseline needed more than 250 GiB of memory 15% of the way through the run, beyond what we may use on the shared server.
  • No aggregation step. The 90% metric says when a host could start aggregating; proof time and proof propagation are not in these runs.
  • Turn-taking and announcements depart from EIP-8334, which sends no metadata to mesh peers. The bundle format and the partial-messages transport are the EIP's.
  • Propagation here appears to be bandwidth-bound. With faster links the timings would likely differ; we have not tested how the duplicate savings change.
  • Requests to non-mesh gossip peers still add about 0.2 duplicates per first copy; switching them off is untested, and at the mesh-3 setting they were what completed delivery.
