Propagating committee proofs: five strategies in the second slot

leansim experiments on branch feature/seacast-proofs · updated 27 November 2026 · Shadow simulations of 16 committees on 3,713 hosts, two proof rounds a slot apart

Summary. Every proof of every committee has to reach every host. We compared five ways of spreading 200 kB proofs: announce and pull, the whole proof on GossipSub, signed Merkle pieces on GossipSub (EIP-8411 style), the same pieces on Waggle, and SEACAST with Reed–Solomon coded chunks on Waggle. Each run now has two rounds: proofs are released 6 s into the run and new proofs from the same producers 12 s later, and all numbers are from the second round. The second round was faster than the first for every strategy except pieces on GossipSub, by 0.22 to 0.41 s for Waggle and 0.76 to 1.25 s for announce and pull at the 95th percentile. Waggle pieces stayed fastest with one and two proofs per committee. With four, announce and pull took the lead (2.05 against 2.87 s) while sending 45% fewer bytes; Waggle kept the shorter tail in one of the two seeds and the only full coverage by 4 s in every run. SEACAST trailed Waggle pieces in every case, and pieces on GossipSub were still the slowest and the heaviest.

1. What we simulate and what we measure

The earlier version of this report measured one round of proofs, released 5 s after the hosts started. A suspicion was that GossipSub had not settled by then. Every run now carries two rounds a 12 s slot apart and reports the second. These runs send no signatures and do no proving.

  • Two rounds. 16 committees, each with 1, 2 or 4 producers, so a round carries 16, 32 or 64 proofs. Every producer releases a 200,000-byte synthetic proof 6 s into the run and a new one from the same host at 18 s, in the middle of the next 12 s slot. The second round's proofs are new objects with their own data, Merkle root and signature; only the connections and each strategy's own state (GossipSub meshes, SEACAST's push relations) carry over. The proof runner has no separate warm-up phase: with a 300 ms heartbeat, GossipSub had run about 20 heartbeats at the first release and 60 at the second. Release times are counted from host start. Runs last 40 s.
  • Each round on its own clock. Times are from that round's release. Bytes are counted from 0.5 s before a release to 0.5 s before the next one (or the end of the run), so traffic of one round that runs past the next release counts in the next round. This matters only for pieces on GossipSub, whose first round was still running at 18 s.
  • The first round reproduces the earlier report. Its 95th percentiles are within 0.16 s of the published single-round numbers for the 22 runs of the four strategies the earlier report had, on the same hosts, links, connections and producers.
  • Coverage. Every host became ready in every round of every run, except one round of pieces on GossipSub in which 2 hosts were not. All strategies but pieces on GossipSub delivered every proof to every host in both rounds; pieces on GossipSub ended their second rounds 33 and 117 deliveries short of 237,568.

Metrics

  • Ready: time from the round's release until a host holds at least one complete proof of that round for every one of the 16 committees, which is what a proposer would need before it can combine them. A host's own proof counts. Median, 95th percentile and last host, over all 3,713 hosts.
  • Ready by 4 s: how many of the 3,713 hosts were ready 4 s after the release.
  • MB sent per host: bytes written to QUIC streams in the round's window, averaged over hosts: proof data including duplicates, signatures, framing and control messages. It leaves out UDP/IP and QUIC packet overhead and retransmissions, so it is not wire bytes. Over the whole run, bytes sent equal bytes received.
  • × one copy: the same bytes divided by one transfer of every proof to every other host, which is 3.20, 6.40 and 12.80 MB per host for 16, 32 and 64 proofs. A strategy that delivers every proof cannot go below 1.

2. The five strategies

  • Announce and pull. A host that holds a complete proof announces it to all its connected peers. A peer requests the proof from the first announcer, keeps one request open per proof, and tries another announcer if the request fails. It announces only after the whole proof has arrived, so each hop adds a full 200 kB transfer.
  • Whole proof on GossipSub. The producer publishes the proof as one message on a global proof topic. Mesh peers forward it when they hold all of it. IDONTWANT is enabled for messages above 1,000 bytes.
  • Pieces, in both piece strategies. The producer cuts the proof into 64 pieces of 3,125 bytes, builds a Merkle tree over them and signs only the root, with one 2,400-byte signature per proof. Every piece travels with its 192-byte Merkle path. A receiver recomputes the root from the piece and the path, verifies the producer's signature over that root once per proof, and holds the proof to it. There is no erasure coding: a host needs all 64 pieces of the same producer.
  • Pieces on GossipSub. The producer publishes the pieces as 64 separate messages on one global topic, and every message carries the signature, because a publisher cannot know which receivers already hold it. A piece message is 3,125 + 192 + 2,400 bytes. A host verifies a piece before it forwards it. This adapts the chunked GossipSub path of the EIP-8411 draft.
  • Pieces on Waggle. The pieces travel on Waggle streams between connected hosts, with no GossipSub involved. A host adds the signature to a piece only while the peer may lack it: it leaves it out once the peer has advertised or sent a piece of that proof, or once it has queued the peer a signed piece. A receiver refuses a piece without a signature while it holds no signed root. A host pushes each piece to at most two peers, picking those with the shortest send queues. A relay pushes only during the 250 ms after its first verified piece; the producer always pushes. Hosts advertise the pieces they hold every 50 ms. From 100 ms after it first hears of a proof, a host requests missing pieces, rarest first, with one open request per peer per proof. A request that is not answered within 550 ms goes to another peer. The policy sits in the application; the vendored Waggle library is unchanged.
  • SEACAST on Waggle. SEACAST v0.12.0 in its tier 4 setting, carried on its Waggle binding: one ObjectPieces per SEACAST message (JOIN, GRAFT, PRUNE, HAVE, WANT, CHUNK) on one topic, nothing on GossipSub. A proof is a group: the producer cuts it into 64 data chunks of 3,125 bytes, adds 64 Reed–Solomon parity chunks (rate 1/2, as SEACAST Lite measured), builds a Merkle tree over the 128 chunks (a 224-byte path each) and signs the root. Any 64 distinct chunks rebuild the proof; the host then claims every chunk, stops asking, re-encodes, checks the root and pushes rebuilt chunks to neighbours still short of 64. Each host keeps a symmetric push relation of 6 to 12 peers (target 8), grafted and pruned like a GossipSub mesh; it pushes a new chunk to at most 2 relation peers (6 on a 1 Gbit/s host) that do not hold it, furthest behind first, and offers it to the others. Holdings and offers are bitmaps. A host asks one announcer per chunk at a time, moves on after 200 ms and parks a peer that has not answered within 400 ms. A byte window of about one round trip of the uplink bounds chunks in flight; queued chunks a peer has meanwhile claimed are cancelled, and pushes to a peer projected to reach 64 without them are skipped. A 1 Gbit/s producer stripes its first copies over every connection. Heartbeat gossip (700 ms) offers recent chunks to a sample of the other links, paced. The root signature goes to a peer at most once per proof: with the first chunk queued to it, and not at all once the peer has sent us a chunk, a holding or an offer of that proof, since it then holds the signed root. Waggle pieces follow the same rule. Simplifications: the per-peer queue never refuses, card 16's guard, scoring and group expiry are left out, and chunks are not compressed (the proof bytes are random).

Every strategy but pieces on GossipSub was faster in the second round, in every case and seed, and the gain is not specific to GossipSub. The whole proof on GossipSub gained 0.50 to 1.36 s at the 95th percentile, but announce and pull, which uses no mesh, gained 0.76 to 1.25 s. SEACAST gained 0.19 to 0.49 s and Waggle pieces 0.22 to 0.41 s.
  • What the second round inherits. The connections have already carried a round of proofs. Our QUIC implementation (quinn-proto 0.11.14, CUBIC) starts every connection with a congestion window of 14,720 bytes and does not shrink it after an idle period, so a connection that moved 200 kB in round 1 starts round 2 with a larger window. Strategies that move a whole proof over few connections (announce and pull, the whole proof) would gain the most from that, and they did. We did not record congestion windows, so this is our explanation, not a measurement.
  • The GossipSub mesh. At the first release GossipSub had run about 20 heartbeats. If an unsettled mesh were the main cost, the whole proof would gain clearly more than announce and pull, which uses no mesh; the two ranges overlap. The whole proof also sent 5 to 11% fewer bytes in round 2, which fits fewer duplicate forwards, but we did not count mesh sizes or duplicates per round.
  • The ranking moves. In round 1 Waggle had the lowest 95th percentile in all six cases, as in the earlier report. In round 2 announce and pull overtook it with four proofs per committee in both seeds, and with one proof per committee the whole proof on GossipSub, third in round 1, tied SEACAST for second.
  • Pieces on GossipSub got slower, by 1.19 and 1.41 s. Their first round was not over: its last host was ready 30.2 s and 32.0 s after the release, past the second release at 12 s, so the two rounds competed for the same uplinks. Every other strategy finished its first round within 9.68 s.

With four proofs per committee announce and pull was fastest at the median and the 95th percentile: 1.43 / 1.48 s and 2.03 / 2.07 s against 2.43 / 2.44 s and 2.87 / 2.88 s for Waggle, at 2.5% above one copy. Its 95th percentile falls from 2.94 to 2.05 s as producers are added, probably because a host needs only one proof per committee and more producers put one closer; we did not measure hop counts. In the second round it ties SEACAST for second with one proof per committee, ties announce and pull for second with two (each ahead in one seed), and with four comes after announce and pull and Waggle, at 3.17 / 3.14 s; its last host needs 6.93 / 6.39 s.

SEACAST was run with its reference constants; nothing was tuned for 200 kB proofs on this network. Several of them are sized for a 1 MiB payload (the window, the 200 ms ask window, the 400 ms promise). In the second round 59 to 77% of its chunk messages were answers to asks rather than pushes, a host parked an unresponsive peer 11 to 103 times per delivered proof, and received 0.91 to 1.26 duplicate chunks for every chunk a delivered proof needs. Its push relation settled at about 6 peers, the low mark, not the target of 8. We have not run the sweep that would show which of these costs the time.

Round 2, seed 42 / seed 43. The proof itself is 200 kB. Overhead is everything else a host sends per proof: duplicates, Merkle paths, signatures, framing and control messages.

  • Announce and pull: 1.025. A host fetches each proof once, so nothing arrives twice; the rest is announcements, requests and framing.
  • Whole proof on GossipSub: 6.3 to 6.8 at the mesh-8 setting, with IDONTWANT enabled. We did not measure how the excess splits between mesh forwards and gossip.
  • Pieces on GossipSub: 8.79 at the mesh-4 setting. A piece message is 5,717 bytes for 3,125 bytes of proof, so one copy of every piece already costs 1.83 times the proof. This round's window also holds the tail of the first round.
  • SEACAST: 2.38 to 2.79. A chunk message carries a 224-byte Merkle path on 3,125 bytes of data. The root signature rides on 5.8 to 7.0% of the chunk messages, 7.0 to 9.6 per host and proof against about 33 connections per host, and makes up 3.6 to 4.2% of the bytes. The rest is mostly duplicate chunks, holdings, offers and asks; we have not split the bytes by message kind.
  • Waggle: 1.76 to 1.86. The Merkle paths add 6.1% to every piece. The signature rides on 12 to 15% of the piece messages, 11.8 to 13.8 per host and proof, and makes up 7.6 to 9.1% of the bytes. Waggle sent 2 to 6% more in round 2 than in round 1; we have not looked into why.

For scale, a 50 Mbit/s uplink moves 6.25 MB per second. With four proofs per committee the whole proof on GossipSub sends 86 MB per host on average and pieces on GossipSub 112 MB, about 14 and 18 s of such an uplink, against 2.1 s for announce and pull and 3.8 s for Waggle. These are averages; we recorded neither queues nor per-host bytes, and the 1 Gbit/s hosts likely send more.

6. A smaller GossipSub mesh

We ran the whole proof with four proofs per committee at both the mesh-8 setting (low 4, target 8, high 12) and the mesh-4 setting (low 3, target 4, high 6), on the same connections, placement and producers. The rows are in the last group of the table in section 4.

In the second round the mesh-4 setting saves about a sixth of the bytes at almost no cost in time. It sends 17% less and its 95th-percentile host is 0.05 s later. In the first round it was 0.45 s later. Its tail is longer in both rounds (last host 9.95 / 7.06 s against 6.93 / 6.39 s in round 2), and it still sends more than five times the one-copy bytes.

7. Conclusion

In a warmed-up network the choice between Waggle pieces and announce and pull depends on the number of proofs and on what matters: the 95th percentile, the tail or the bytes.

  • Announce and pull is the cheapest, within 2.5% of one copy, and with four proofs per committee also the fastest at the median and the 95th percentile. With one proof per committee it is 1.51 s behind Waggle at the 95th percentile, and its last host varies more from seed to seed.
  • Waggle pieces (64 pieces with their Merkle paths, the root signature sent to a peer only while it may lack it, a short push followed by rarest-first requests) are the fastest with one and two proofs per committee and the only strategy with every host ready by 4 s in every run, at 1.8 to 1.9 times the one-copy bytes.
  • The whole proof on GossipSub gains, with announce and pull, the most from the warm network, but sends more than six times the one-copy bytes. SEACAST as specified trails Waggle pieces; it is the one to tune if erasure coding matters, for example against withholding producers, which none of these runs model. Pieces on GossipSub with the signature in every piece do not keep up with a 12 s slot at four proofs per committee.

The earlier single-round report found Waggle fastest in all six cases. That still holds for the first round of these runs; the second round changes the result with four proofs per committee. These are proof transfers only: neither round carries signature traffic or proving, which would compete for the same uplinks in a real slot.
